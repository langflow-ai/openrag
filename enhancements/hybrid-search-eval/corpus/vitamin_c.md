# Vitamin C (Ascorbic Acid)

## Functions and Intake

Vitamin C, also called ascorbic acid, is a water-soluble vitamin essential for immune function and collagen synthesis. Because humans cannot synthesise it, it must come from the diet. Collagen is the structural protein of skin, tendons, blood vessels and gums, and its formation depends on ascorbic acid as a cofactor for the enzymes that stabilise the collagen triple helix. Vitamin C also supports immune function by accumulating in white blood cells, where it helps them fight infection. The recommended daily intake is 65-90mg for adults. Rich sources include citrus fruits, bell peppers and broccoli, and cooking can reduce the content because the vitamin is heat sensitive.

## Deficiency and Supplementation

Severe deficiency causes scurvy. Symptoms include fatigue, gum disease with swelling and bleeding, and skin lesions, along with poor wound healing and joint pain. Scurvy was historically common among sailors on long voyages without fresh produce. Supplementation is often promoted for colds; trials suggest it does not prevent colds in the general population but may modestly shorten their duration, and regular supplementation seems to help most in people under heavy physical stress.

## Contrast with Vitamin D

Unlike vitamin C, vitamin D is fat-soluble and stored in body tissue, so excess intake can accumulate. It is produced in skin through sun exposure and is also found in oily fish and fortified foods. Deficiency causes rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults, both involving weakened, poorly mineralised bones.

## Antioxidant Mechanisms

Ascorbic acid acts as an antioxidant by donating electrons to neutralise reactive oxygen species, a process called free radical scavenging. It also regenerates oxidised vitamin E, helping protect cell membranes from oxidative damage.
